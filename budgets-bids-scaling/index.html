Budgets, bids and scaling

Lesson 3 set the most you can pay for a sale: $27 in its example. Lesson 7 built the account. Now spend the money: choose how Meta bids, see why each extra dollar buys less, and grow without paying more for a sale than it’s worth.

Step 1 of 6

Five bid strategies, three kinds

A bid strategy tells Meta how to spend your budget in the auction. Meta sorts its five strategies into three kinds.

Spend-based strategies spend the budget and get what they can: Highest volume goes for the most conversions, Highest value for the most valuable purchases. Goal-based strategies aim for an average: a cost per result or a return on ad spend. The manual one, Bid cap, sets the most Meta may bid in any auction.

Older guides say “lowest cost” and “cost cap”. Meta’s current help page doesn’t use those names. The closest current ones are Highest volume and Cost per result goal.

Meta saysThree groups: spend-based (Highest volume, Highest value), goal-based (Cost per result goal, ROAS goal) and manual (Bid cap). For goal-based bidding, Meta recommends “50–100 or more weekly conversions”, and it guarantees neither goal.

SoPick the strategy by what you want held steady: the budget, the average result, or the bid.

Step 2 of 6

Each extra dollar buys less

The cheapest sales come first. Meta’s auction favours ads it predicts people will act on, so a small budget goes to the people most likely to buy. A bigger budget has to reach further, to people less likely to buy, and each of their sales costs more.

That gives a curve that bends. In this example the first $100 a day buys 6.3 sales. At $500 a day, the last $100 buys 4.3. At $2,000 a day, the last $100 buys less than one.

The curve is the course’s model, with made-up numbers. Your account has its own, and you only see it by changing the budget.

Meta saysThe auction picks the ad with the highest “total value”, which combines the bid, estimated action rates and ad quality, so a more relevant ad can beat a higher bid.

SoDoubling the budget won’t double the sales. Expect every step up to cost more per sale.

Step 3 of 6

Watch what the extra sales cost

Ads Manager shows the average cost per result: everything you spent divided by every result. When you raise the budget, the average rises slowly, because the cheap sales from before are still in it.

What matters is what the extra sales cost. Slide the spend up. On this curve the extra sales pass the $27 breakeven at $588 a day, while the average is still $19.84. The average doesn’t reach $27 until $1,318 a day, and by then the profit is gone.

Profit peaks where the extra sales cost exactly breakeven: about $212 a day, at $588. Past that point, every extra sale loses money even while the average looks healthy.

Meta saysCost per result is the average cost per result, and what counts as a result depends on the performance goal.

Check yoursTake two weeks at different budgets. Work out (new spend − old spend) ÷ (new sales − old sales). That’s what your extra sales cost. Compare it with your breakeven from lesson 3.

SoKeep raising the budget while the extra sales cost less than your breakeven. Stop when they cost more, whatever the average says.

Step 4 of 6

Raise the budget in steps you can read

Meta counts a large budget change as a possible significant edit, which can send the ad set back into learning. Its example: $100 to $1,000 a day may count; $100 to $101 likely won’t.

A big jump has a second problem: it mixes cheap and expensive extra sales into one number. In the top lane, going from $100 to $1,000 buys extra sales at $25 each on average, which looks fine against $27. But the last of them cost $41, and profit ends at $138 a day.

The bottom lane raises the budget a step at a time and reads what each step’s extra sales cost. When a step comes in at $28, over breakeven, it gets undone. That lane ends at $500 a day and $208 a day in profit.

Meta gives no safe percentage. The course’s rule: make each step small enough that you can read what it bought, and give it a few days before the next. The step sizes here are Example numbers, not a rule.

Meta saysA change to the budget, bid, goal or spending limit may count as a significant edit, depending on its size: $100 to $1,000 may count, while $100 to $101 likely won’t.

SoScale in steps and judge each one by its extra sales. Keep the steps that pay and undo the one that doesn’t.

Step 5 of 6

New concepts move the curve

More budget slides you further along the same curve, to buyers who cost more. New concepts do something budget can’t: they reach people your current ads don’t move, so the whole curve shifts up and out.

In this example both options spend $1,000 a day. With more budget alone you get 42 sales, and the extra ones cost $41. With new concepts the same $1,000 buys 51 sales, the extra ones cost $26, and the point where extra sales pass $27 moves from $588 to $1,058 a day.

That’s lesson 1’s idea at budget scale: different ads find different people. Lesson 4 shows how to make concepts that are genuinely different.

SoWhen extra sales get expensive, the fix is usually new concepts, not more budget.

Step 6 of 6

Goals and caps are brakes

A Cost per result goal tells Meta the average you want. In this model, Meta spends while the average stays at or under your goal, then stops, even if budget is left.

Set a $20 goal on a $1,500 budget and delivery stops near $606 a day, close to the most profitable spend. Set the goal at breakeven, $27, and it spends $1,318 for about zero profit. Above about $29, this budget never reaches the goal, so all of it gets spent.

A goal can’t make sales cheaper. It only stops Meta buying the expensive ones. Bid cap does the same one auction at a time.

The course’s view: prefer a Cost per result goal at your target over Highest volume, because it protects the margin on every sale. It works best once the ad set gets the weekly conversions Meta recommends for goal-based bidding.

Meta saysA Cost per result goal keeps the average cost near a target, and a ROAS goal keeps the average return near one. Meta guarantees neither. Bid cap sets the maximum bid in every auction and is meant for advertisers who know their predicted conversion rates.

SoSet the goal at your target, not at breakeven. A goal at breakeven lets Meta spend your profit.

The whole thing

Five lines to remember

  1. Bid strategies come in three kinds: spend-based (Highest volume, Highest value), goal-based (Cost per result goal, ROAS goal) and manual (Bid cap).
  2. More budget buys pricier sales: each extra dollar buys less than the one before.
  3. Judge a budget increase by what the extra sales cost, not by the average.
  4. Raise budgets in steps you can read, and undo a step whose extra sales cost more than your breakeven.
  5. New concepts shift the curve out. Goals and caps only stop the spending.
