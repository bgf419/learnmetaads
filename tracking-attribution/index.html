Tracking and attribution

Every number in lessons 2 and 3 starts with Meta hearing about a sale. This lesson follows a sale from your checkout to Ads Manager, shows where it gets lost or counted twice, and separates the sales your ads were near from the sales your ads caused.

Step 1 of 6

How a sale reaches Meta: the pixel

Meta can only count a sale it hears about. The Meta Pixel is a snippet of code on your site. When a visitor does something, it sends an event from their browser to Meta: PageView when a page loads, AddToCart when they add a product, Purchase when they buy.

Some of those events never arrive. Ad blockers stop the pixel, people close the tab before the thank-you page loads, and browsers limit what tracking code can do. The sale still happened. Meta just never heard about it.

Every purchase that goes missing makes your cost per sale look worse than it really is.

Meta saysFrom 2025-09-02, custom conversions (results you define from your own events) that suggest a health condition or financial status are flagged and blocked from new campaigns.

SoThe pixel only sees what the browser lets it send. Some real sales never reach Meta.

Step 2 of 6

The Conversions API, and counting once

The Conversions API (CAPI) sends the same events from your server, the computer that runs your store, straight to Meta. It doesn’t run in the visitor’s browser, so the browser problems from step 1 don’t touch it.

Run both and Meta hears most purchases twice. To count each sale once, it deduplicates: when a browser event and a server event carry the same event ID and the same event name, and arrive within 48 hours, Meta keeps one.

Leave out the event ID and Meta can’t reliably pair them. In the worst case every sale both sides saw counts twice, so your cost per sale looks lower and your ROAS higher than they really are.

Meta saysRun the Conversions API alongside the Pixel. Deduplication needs the pixel’s eventID to equal the server’s event_id, matching event names, and arrival within 48 hours. Without them, Meta falls back on the event name plus fbp and/or external_id, which works mainly when the browser event arrives first.

Meta saysThe Conversions API Gateway is a no-code setup from Events Manager that runs in your own cloud account and sets event_id automatically.

SoSend purchases from the browser and the server, with the same event ID on both, so every sale counts exactly once.

Step 3 of 6

Match quality

To credit a sale to an ad, Meta has to tie the event to a person with a Meta account. It does that with the customer details you send along with the event: an email address, a phone number, a click ID and so on. That’s matching.

Event Match Quality (EMQ) scores how well your events match, out of 10. Meta suggests 6.0 or higher. The score rises as more of your events carry customer details and as each one carries more of them.

Not every detail counts the same. Email and click ID are strong. Name, city and zip code are weak. Toggle them below. The meter is an illustration: its weights are made up to show the direction, not Meta’s formula.

Meta saysEMQ is scored out of 10 from which customer details arrive, their quality, and the share of events matched to a Meta account. Meta suggests an EMQ of 6.0 or higher; the Help Center shows it as Poor, OK, Good or Great.

Meta saysEmail and click ID rate high as match keys; name, city and zip rate low.

SoSend email and click ID with every purchase you can. Each detail you add helps Meta find the buyer behind the sale.

Step 4 of 6

Attribution windows

Attribution decides which sales Meta credits to an ad. The attribution setting is a window: how long after someone clicks or sees your ad a purchase still counts.

In Meta’s Insights API the default is 7-day click plus 1-day view. A purchase counts if it comes within 7 days of a click on the ad, or within 1 day of seeing it without clicking. Drag the purchase day and switch between a click and a view.

The rules changed in 2026. Check the dates below before you compare this year’s numbers with last year’s.

Meta saysSince 2026-01-12, the 7-day view and 28-day view windows return no data in the Ads Insights API, on every version.

Meta saysFrom late March 2026, click-through counts link clicks only. Conversions after shares, saves and other non-link clicks move to engaged view, renamed engage-through attribution. A video engaged view now takes 5 seconds instead of 10.

ReportedA trade publication reports the windows still returned are 1-day click, 7-day click, 28-day click, 1-day engaged view and 1-day view.

SoEvery sale count comes with a window. Change the window and the number changes, even though the sales didn’t.

Step 5 of 6

Counted is not caused

Attribution counts sales that happened near an ad. It can’t tell you which ones the ad caused. Some of those buyers were already on their way: they’d bought before, or they were going to search for you anyway.

Here 100 people saw or clicked an ad and bought inside the window, so Meta counts 100 sales for $4,000 of ads: $40 a sale. Suppose 60 would have bought anyway. Only 40 bought because of the ad, so each extra sale really cost $4,000 ÷ 40 = $100.

Slide the share who would have bought anyway. Ads Manager’s number never moves. The real cost does.

Meta saysMeta’s lift glossary defines incremental conversions as those a test estimates the ads caused.

ReportedPractitioners report that Meta’s incremental attribution setting credits only conversions Meta estimates would not have happened without the ad, needs a Maximize conversions or value goal, applies to website Sales campaigns, excludes bid controls and is locked after launch.

SoA counted sale is not a caused sale. The gap between them is the part of your results the ads didn’t earn.

Step 6 of 6

Lift tests

To measure what your ads caused, run an experiment. Split people at random into two groups. The test group can see your ads; the control group doesn’t. Whatever the test group buys beyond the control group is the lift: the sales the ads caused.

Because the split is random, the ads are the only difference between the groups. Slide the true lift, then press Run again. The measured gap bounces around the truth because each group is a sample. Switch to a small test and the bounce gets bigger, big enough to show a lift that isn’t there or hide one that is.

Meta saysIncrementality experiments such as Conversion Lift are the best way to tell whether ads caused conversions. Where attribution and lift results disagree, calibrate attribution against the lift results.

ReportedGeoLift is Meta’s open-source R package for tests run by region, and Robyn is Meta’s open-source marketing mix model, calibrated with lift and geo results. A practitioner guide advises running GeoLift’s power analysis first and planning for a 5–10% detectable effect, 4–8 weeks and 80% power.

Check yoursIn Events Manager, find your Purchase event’s Event Match Quality and compare it with Meta’s suggested 6.0. Then confirm your browser and server purchase events carry the same event ID.

SoAttribution tells you what Meta counted. A lift test with a random control group tells you what your ads added.

The whole thing

Five lines to remember

  1. The pixel reports sales from the visitor’s browser, and some of them never arrive.
  2. The Conversions API sends the same events from your server, and a shared event ID lets Meta count each sale once.
  3. Event Match Quality is scored out of 10, Meta suggests 6.0 or higher, and email and click ID count for the most.
  4. A sale counts only inside the attribution window, and the Insights API default is 7-day click plus 1-day view.
  5. Counted is not caused, and only a test with a random control group measures what your ads added.
