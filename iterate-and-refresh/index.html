Lesson 6 · Creative strategy · 10 min

Iterate, explore, refresh

Lesson 4 showed how to find concepts and lesson 5 how to build each ad. This lesson covers what comes after launch: reading a winner, making more of it, finding the next one and keeping new ads coming every week.

Step 1 of 6

Read the winner

When an ad wins, the urge is to copy it exactly. First work out why it won. Every ad is a set of parts: the audience, the angle, the offer, the ad type, the hook and the talent (the person on screen).

Compare the winner with the ads that lost. A part it shares with losing ads can’t be what made it win. A part that’s new to the account is your best clue.

Tap each part to check it. In lesson 4 you wrote a hypothesis before making an ad. A winner is a hypothesis that held, and this is how you find which part of it held.

SoKeep the parts that explain the win. Everything else is free to change.

Step 2 of 6

Exploit: variants of a winner

Exploiting a winner means making variants: new ads that keep who it’s for and why they’d care, and change one part each. The course’s rule is 5 to 10 variants per winner.

There are four kinds of change: a new hook, a new message (the same point in different words), a new ad type (the same message as a static, a side-by-side or an animation) and new talent (the same script, a different person).

Change one part per variant. As in lesson 5, one change at a time tells you what moved the numbers. Drag the slider to fan out the winner.

SoEvery winner should become 5 to 10 new ads, each changing one part.

Step 3 of 6

Explore: new concepts

Exploring means testing new concepts, as far from your current winners as you can get. Variants of proven winners bring in most of the steady sales. But every winner tires in the end, and each new concept that wins opens up a fresh set of variants to make.

This example launches 20 new ads a week, and a new concept is less likely to win than a variant. Drag the share that are new concepts. With too little exploring, sales look fine for a few weeks, then fall as today’s winners tire with nothing ready to replace them. With too much, sales hold but lower, because fewer ads are proven variants.

The course starts at 60% to 70% variants and 30% to 40% new concepts. Explore more when you have few winners or they tire fast.

SoExploring is how next month’s winners get found. Keep about a third of new ads for new concepts.

Step 4 of 6

Spot fatigue

An ad that keeps running gets shown to the same people again and again. Frequency is the average number of times each person has seen it. As it climbs, the ad gets less done for each dollar, and cost per result creeps up.

Daily numbers bounce around, and one bad day is noise. Switch to weeks to see the trend, and compare the ad with its own first two weeks, not with other ads.

Meta saysAds Manager has “creative fatigue” recommendations for when an audience has seen the same creative too many times. Ad relevance diagnostics need 500 or more impressions, and a drop can mean the creative or the audience needs a refresh.

ReportedOne agency (ovrdrv.com) reports that Meta’s “creative limited” label means cost per result has risen against your past ads by less than double, and “creative fatigue” means it has doubled. Meta hasn’t confirmed these thresholds.

Check yoursTake your longest-running ad. Write down its cost per result and frequency for each week it has run, and compare the last two weeks with its first two.

SoRefresh an ad on a trend, not a bad day: cost per result rising week after week against its own start.

Step 5 of 6

Ship a batch every week

Winners tire, so new ones have to keep coming. Ship a batch of new ads every week. Each batch adds a few winners that carry sales for a few weeks, then fade as newer batches take over.

Size the batch by working back from the winners you need. If about 15% of ads become keepers (winners worth keeping), 4 new winners a month takes 27 ads: 4 ÷ 0.15, rounded up. In this example, 4 a month is what holds sales flat.

Our dataAbout 15% of ecommerce ads in the Library become keepers, the rate used here. (Lesson 1, from 8,885 Ad Library advertisers.)

ReportedMotion, a creative analytics tool, calls 10 to 15 ads a month outdated and argues for 50 to 100. It also suggests 10 to 20 variations per ad set.

SoSet the weekly batch from the winners you need, and don’t skip a week.

Step 6 of 6

The brief builder

A brief is the one-page plan for an ad: who it’s for, what it says, why it should work and how you’ll judge it. Write it before anyone films anything.

Pick the concept from lesson 4’s lists, add three hooks from lesson 5, choose the proof and the call to action (CTA), and the hypothesis line writes itself. Change any field and the brief updates. New concept picks a random one and leaves the hooks for you to write.

A brief that can’t say why the ad should win isn’t ready. Fill in the “because” before you send it.

Check yoursWrite next week’s three briefs: two variants of your best ad, each changing one part, and one new concept.

SoEvery ad starts as a brief with a hypothesis. That’s how a batch turns into lessons, not just results.

The whole thing

Five lines to remember

  1. Before you copy a winner, find which parts made it win by comparing it with the ads that lost.
  2. Make 5 to 10 variants of each winner, changing one part at a time.
  3. Keep about a third of new ads for new concepts, so fresh winners are ready when today’s tire.
  4. Judge fatigue by the weekly trend against the ad’s own first weeks, not by one bad day.
  5. Ship a batch every week, sized from the winners you need, and start every ad with a brief.

Where the numbers come from

  • Example numbers are made up to show the method: the winner and its clues, the variants, the fatigue curve and the weekly batches. The 12-week simulation in step 3 assumes 20 new ads a week; a new concept wins 5% of the time; a variant of a proven concept wins 20% of the time until that concept’s variant ideas run out; each new concept that wins makes room for about six more winning variants; and a winner’s sales fade over five weeks. At the starting split, about 15% of new ads become winners, close to lesson 1’s keeper rate. Change them and the best split moves; the pattern stays: too little exploring runs dry later.
