What you can afford to pay

Lesson 2 ended with a $40 cost per sale on a $60 order, a ROAS of 1.5. Whether that’s good lives in your costs, not in Ads Manager. This lesson works out the most you can pay for a sale and still make money, then turns it into a target you can check every day.

Step 1 of 6

Where $60 goes

A $60 order isn’t $60 you can spend. Before any ad money, the product costs $18, shipping and packing cost $9, the card processor takes $2, and returns and discounts eat about $4 an order on average.

What’s left is $27. That’s the order’s contribution: what it contributes to paying for ads and making a profit. As a share of the price it’s the contribution margin: $27 ÷ $60 = 45%.

Every dollar you pay Meta for that sale comes out of the $27.

SoYour ads can only spend what an order leaves behind. Know that number before you judge any cost per sale.

Step 2 of 6

Breakeven

If a sale costs exactly $27 in ads, you make nothing and lose nothing. That’s your breakeven CPA (cost per acquisition, lesson 2’s cost per sale).

In ROAS terms, breakeven is price ÷ contribution: $60 ÷ $27 = 2.22. The same thing is 1 ÷ margin: 1 ÷ 0.45 = 2.22. Below 2.22, every sale loses money. Above it, every sale makes some.

Lesson 2’s ad ran at a ROAS of 1.5, so at a 45% margin it loses money on every order. Now slide the margin down to 20%: breakeven ROAS jumps to 5.0. A thin-margin product needs a far better ad just to break even.

SoBreakeven ROAS = 1 ÷ margin. A ROAS that pays well on one product loses money on another.

Step 3 of 6

Profit per sale

Now put a price on the sale. Profit per sale = contribution − cost per sale. At lesson 2’s $40, each order loses $13. At $20, each order makes $7.

Slide the cost per sale. While it stays under $27, the gold part of the bar is profit. Past $27 it turns red: you’re paying Meta to lose money on every order.

Small gaps add up. $5 a sale is $500 on every 100 sales, in either direction.

SoEvery dollar of cost per sale under breakeven is a dollar of profit. Every dollar over it is a dollar lost, on every order.

Step 4 of 6

Customers come back

So far each customer buys once. Some come back, and their next order costs you no ads. Here, out of every 100 first-time buyers, 30 order a second time within 12 months and 10 of those order a third time. Half of the second orders arrive in the first 90 days.

Each repeat order leaves another $27. Spread across all 100 buyers, that raises what you can pay to win the first order. Pick how long you’re willing to wait to earn it back: that’s your payback window.

Two warnings. Count only repeat profit you’ve measured from your own past customers, never what you hope for. And the cash comes back late: pay $37.80 for a customer today and it takes a year to earn it back, so you need money to wait with.

SoRepeat buyers raise what a first sale can cost, but only by repeat profit you’ve measured, and only if you can wait for the cash.

Step 5 of 6

Meta’s ROAS vs your whole business

Meta’s ROAS counts only the sales Meta credits to your ads, under its attribution setting: the rules for how long after a click or a view a sale still counts. Lesson 10 covers those rules.

Your bank account counts every sale: from Meta, from other ad channels, from email, from people who typed in your address. MER (marketing efficiency ratio) is total revenue ÷ total ad spend, across every channel.

Here Meta shows a ROAS of 3.0, which looks safe against a 2.22 breakeven. The whole business runs at an MER of 2.5. At a 45% margin, $30,000 of sales leaves $13,500, and $12,000 of ads takes most of it: $1,500 is left.

Meta saysIn Meta’s Insights API, the default attribution setting is 7-day click plus 1-day view.

SoSet your targets on MER and profit. Use Meta’s ROAS to compare ads against each other inside Meta.

Step 6 of 6

Your targets

Type in one product’s numbers. Returns and discounts go in as a share of the price: $4 on a $60 order is 6.67%.

Leave repeat profit at 0 until you’ve measured it. Then add only the repeat profit per buyer that comes back inside a payback window you can afford.

Breakeven is the line you must not cross. Your target sits below it by a profit cushion: the share of each sale’s contribution you want to keep. A 20% cushion on $27 means aiming for $21.60 or less.

Check yoursIn Ads Manager, write your breakeven CPA and target CPA next to the Cost per purchase column. Any ad over breakeven with real spend is losing money on every sale.

SoTwo numbers run the account: the breakeven you can’t cross, and the target you aim for.

The whole thing

Five lines to remember

  1. Contribution is what an order leaves after product, shipping, fees and returns, and it’s the most a one-time sale can pay for ads.
  2. Breakeven CPA equals your contribution, and breakeven ROAS is 1 ÷ margin, so thin margins need a far higher ROAS.
  3. Profit per sale is contribution minus cost per sale, and it multiplies across every order you buy.
  4. Repeat buyers raise what a first sale can cost, but count only repeat profit you’ve measured and only if you can wait for the cash.
  5. Set targets on MER and profit, and use Meta’s ROAS to compare ads inside Meta.

Where the numbers come from

  • Example numbers in every step are made up so the arithmetic is easy to follow: a $60 order with $18 product cost, $9 shipping and packing, $2 payment fees and $4 returns and discounts; the repeat rates in step 4; and the month in step 5. Use your own.
  • Attribution default: in Meta’s Insights API, the default attribution setting is 7-day click plus 1-day view (Ad insights reference, v25.0). Lesson 10 covers attribution in full.
  • Contribution margin, breakeven, payback window and MER are standard business arithmetic, not Meta settings. Setting targets on MER and profit, and using Meta’s ROAS to compare ads, is this course’s advice.
