Testing without fooling yourself

Lesson 8 spent the money. Now read what it bought. Small numbers lie, spend lands unevenly, and the first numbers to arrive aren’t the ones that pay. Here’s how much data a verdict needs, simple rules for what to cut and keep, and how to make each test teach the next one.

Step 1 of 6

Small numbers lie

Here are two identical ads: same creative, same audience, and the same true conversion rate. 2.5% of the people who click buy. Each ad gets 40 clicks, so each should get about one sale.

Press Run again. Most runs, one ad looks better than the other, sometimes three times better. Nothing differs but luck. In this example one ad comes out ahead in about 7 runs out of 10.

Real tests look exactly like this in their first days. A gap between 3 sales and 1 sale is not a finding.

SoA gap between two small numbers is usually luck. Don’t pick a winner from a handful of sales.

Step 2 of 6

How much data is enough

Every count is noisy. A handy rule: a count of n can be off by about 2 ÷ √n of itself, either way. At 25 sales that’s ±40%. At 100 sales it’s ±20%.

Cost per sale is spend divided by sales, so it carries the same noise. In this example the ad shows $20 a sale against a $27 breakeven. At 25 sales the true cost could be anywhere from $14 to $33, so you can’t tell which side of $27 it’s on. It takes about 60 sales before the whole range sits under $27.

Clicks pile up much faster than sales, which makes them tempting to judge on. But sales are what you pay for, so judge cost per sale on sales.

Check yoursBefore you call a test, count the sales behind each number. Work out 2 ÷ √sales. If the range it gives crosses your breakeven, it’s too early.

SoJudge on sales, not clicks, and wait until the whole range clears your breakeven.

Step 3 of 6

Uneven spend means untested, not lost

Put ten ads in one ad set and Meta won’t split the budget evenly. As lesson 1 showed, it spends a little on each new ad to find out, then feeds the ads that get early results. Spend piles onto a few ads fast.

In this example, two ads take 53% of the $1,306. Three ads spent less than $44, twice the $22 target. Ad J spent $8. That isn’t a losing ad. It’s an ad nobody has tested.

Don’t file a low-spend ad under “didn’t work”. If you still believe in it, put it in your next test batch. If you need a clean head-to-head, use Meta’s A/B test tool.

Meta saysThe A/B test tool compares up to five ad variants. Test one variable at a time, and don’t test by toggling ad sets on and off.

SoAn ad that barely spent has told you nothing. Call it untested, and don’t count it as a loss.

Step 4 of 6

Cut and keep rules tied to your target

You can’t wait for 60 sales on every ad. You need rules that cut clear losers early and leave everything else alone. These are the course’s rules of thumb, set against your target cost per sale from lesson 3:

Turn it off once it has spent 2× your target and still costs more than 2× your target per sale. No sales counts as more. Keep it once it has 3 or more sales at or under your target. Everything else is too early: leave it running.

“Keep” means keep it running, not scale it. Three sales can’t prove an ad is good (step 2). They only show it’s earning its spend so far.

Move the target. With the same ten ads, a $15 target keeps nothing and cuts six. A $60 target keeps four and cuts nothing. The rules only work if the target is your real one.

SoCut on spend against your target, keep on sales at your target, and leave the rest alone.

Step 5 of 6

Early signals tell you what to fix

Sales are slow to count. Other numbers settle much sooner, because the events behind them are far more common.

Hook rate is 3-second plays ÷ impressions: did the opening stop people? Hold rate is ThruPlays ÷ 3-second plays: did they keep watching? Link CTR, from lesson 2, is link clicks ÷ impressions: did they act?

Slide through two weeks of a test ad at $20 a day. Its hook and hold rates are steady on day 1 and its link CTR by day 5. Its cost per sale is still a guess on day 14, with about 8 sales.

So use the early numbers to choose what to iterate (lesson 6). A weak hook rate calls for a new opening. A good hook with a weak hold calls for a new middle. Don’t cut an ad on them: cuts go by spend against your target (step 4).

Meta says3-second video plays count plays of at least 3 seconds, or nearly the whole video if it’s shorter. ThruPlays count plays to the end or of at least 15 seconds. Link CTR is link clicks divided by impressions.

ReportedSegwise and Jetfuel define hook rate as 3-second plays ÷ impressions and hold rate as ThruPlays ÷ 3-second plays. Some use 15-second views instead of ThruPlays.

SoEarly numbers tell you what to fix. Only sales tell you what to keep.

Step 6 of 6

Write every test down

A test nobody writes down teaches you once, then gets forgotten. A log turns each test into a line you can build on: what you thought, what happened, what you learned and what you’ll make next.

Read the example log. No single row proves much. But the parent concept and the ice hook each looked good alone, and together they made the best ad in the log. That only happens because someone wrote the first two down.

Log the honest result, including “too early”. An untested ad goes back in the queue, not in the bin.

Start yoursStart a log with your last 5 tests. One line each: hypothesis, result, what you learned, what you’ll make next.

SoLearnings only compound when they’re written down. The log is how this week’s test makes next week’s brief better.

The whole thing

Five lines to remember

  1. A gap between two small numbers is usually luck.
  2. A count of n is off by about 2 ÷ √n. Judge cost per sale on sales, and wait until the range clears your breakeven.
  3. An ad that barely spent is untested, not lost.
  4. Cut on spend against your target, keep on sales at your target, and leave the rest running.
  5. Hook rate, hold rate and CTR tell you what to fix. Write every test down so the learnings compound.
