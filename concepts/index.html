Concepts: who, what and why

Lesson 1 showed why variety wins the auction: different ads reach different people. This lesson covers what to vary. You’ll split one product into many reasons to buy, place each buyer on a ladder of awareness, and combine the pieces into concepts you can test.

Step 1 of 6

One product, many reasons to buy

Take one product: a $60 insulated water bottle. Six different people might buy it, and each one has a different reason.

That reason is the angle: why this particular person would care. The product doesn’t change. The angle changes what the ad says first, what it shows, and who stops scrolling for it.

Tap each buyer. The saver hears price. The commuter hears speed. The trend-chaser hears fear of missing out (FOMO): buy now or lose the chance.

SoEvery product has several reasons to buy. Each reason is a different ad for a different person.

Step 2 of 6

How aware is the buyer?

Buyers also differ in how much they already know. The copywriter Eugene Schwartz sorted them into five levels in his 1966 book Breakthrough Advertising: unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware and most aware.

The level decides where your ad has to start. Someone unaware needs the problem named before any product makes sense. Someone most aware only needs a reason to buy today.

Move the slider and watch the opening line change. A cold audience, people who have never heard of you, sits on the bottom three rungs: they can’t be product-aware yet.

SoStart where the buyer is. The less they know, the further back your first line has to begin.

Step 3 of 6

The concept grid

A concept is one complete idea for an ad: who it’s for (the audience), why they’d care (the angle), what they get for acting (the offer) and the shape of the ad (the ad type).

Ad types include a UGC video (user-generated content: a customer filming themselves), a static image, a side-by-side comparison, an unboxing, a carousel and an animation.

Short lists multiply fast. Four audiences, five angles, three offers and six ad types make 4 × 5 × 3 × 6 = 360 concepts. Spin the reels to draw one.

SoThe grid isn’t a to-do list. It’s a map of combinations, so you can see which ones you’ve never tried.

Step 4 of 6

Different, not just new

A new background colour or a swapped word makes a new ad, but not a new concept. It still says the same thing, for the same reason, to the same person.

Lesson 1 showed why variety matters: different ads reach different people. Ads that say the same thing to the same people can’t do that. Change the audience, the angle or the ad type, and you’re talking to someone new.

The course’s rule: make each new concept as different from your last ones as you can, and don’t copy competitors. An ad that looks like the category leader’s goes after the same people with a weaker version of their idea.

ReportedAgencies report that since Meta’s Andromeda ad-retrieval update (late 2024), near-identical ads also get grouped and share one ticket into the auction. Meta has published no such rule.

Meta saysBuild a variety of creative so Meta’s AI can match the right message to each person.

SoCount concepts, not ads. Twenty versions of one idea are still one idea.

Step 5 of 6

Where angles come from

Don’t invent angles at your desk. Look in four places, the 4 Cs: the company (what you make and why), the consumer (what buyers say), the category (what every competitor claims, and what they all skip) and the culture (what people are talking about right now).

Start with the consumer, and start with your reviews. Buyers tell you why they bought, in words you can put straight into an ad.

Tap each review to sort it into its angle.

Check yoursPull 50 of your own reviews and tag each one with an angle. The biggest bucket is your strongest lead, and its exact phrases are ready-made hooks.

SoYour customers have already written your angles. Sort what they say before you write a word.

Step 6 of 6

Write the hypothesis

Before you make the ad, write down what you expect and how you’ll know. One sentence does it:

For [audience] at [awareness], [angle] will beat [current angle], because [insight]. We’ll know if [metric] beats [benchmark].

The insight is the “because”: what’s true about this buyer that makes the angle work. Without it, a win teaches you nothing you can use again.

Test the message before you pay for video. The course’s method: put 10 to 20 angles on simple branded statics, a product image with one headline each, and run them side by side. The angles that earn clicks become your next videos.

SoA concept without a hypothesis is a guess. Write the sentence, then make the ad.

The whole thing

Five lines to remember

  1. An angle is the reason one person would care, and one product has many.
  2. Awareness sets the first line: the less someone knows, the further back your ad has to start.
  3. A concept is an audience, an angle, an offer and an ad type, and four short lists make hundreds of them.
  4. A new colour or a new word is the same concept again, so count concepts, not ads.
  5. Find angles in your customers’ own words, then write a hypothesis before you make the ad.
